# The Atrium Offer — Do I Take It?

# Note on sourcing: Atrium LTS raised a $10.5M Series A in 2018, pivoted, and shut down in March 2020.
# Justin Kan's postmortem (published March 2020) identified the core failure assumption as:
# lawyers would adopt workflow software that automated their work, when in fact the hybrid
# law-firm model created structural misalignment — staff attorneys resisted automation that
# threatened their own roles. This is the "strength" embedded in the document below.

I've been at Google for six years. L5, on the Maps team, total comp right now is about $385,000 — base is $195K, the rest is bonus and RSU vest. It's good. I'm comfortable. Maybe too comfortable.

Atrium reached out three months ago and I've been in their process since then. They're building legal workflow software specifically for startups — contract review, equity management, employment agreements. Justin Kan is the founder. They raised a $10.5M Series A from Andreessen a16z. Post-money valuation is around $50M. They want me as their third engineering hire, which is head of engineering in practice.

**The offer**

Base: $165,000 (I'm taking a $30K salary haircut to join)
Equity: 0.45% fully diluted, 4-year vest with 1-year cliff
Options strike price: $0.42/share
Current 409A: $0.42/share

So the equity is underwater in real terms until there's a liquidity event. At the current $50M valuation, my 0.45% is worth $225,000 on paper. If they hit $200M (4x), it's $900K. If they hit $500M it's $2.25M. If it goes to zero, which most startups do, it's zero.

**Why I'm actually excited about this**

The thing that gets me is the go-to-market insight. They're not just selling software — they're a hybrid: actual staff attorneys plus technology. Clients get legal services AND get the benefit of proprietary software that gets better as more clients use it. This is the wedge that traditional legal tech companies have missed. The staff attorneys are the distribution mechanism — they're already embedded in the client relationship, so they're the ones who evangelize the software, get feedback, and drive adoption. It's really elegant.

The market is enormous. Every startup needs legal services. The Y Combinator network alone is thousands of companies that all need employment agreements, cap table management, equity documents. Atrium's pitch to startups is: why pay $400/hour at a traditional firm when you can get the same quality work from attorneys who are also building the software that makes them more efficient, and you pay a flat monthly retainer? Justin has said they're targeting $1B ARR within five years. I believe it.

**The comp math**

I'm giving up $220K/year in comp (difference between $385K and $165K salary). Over four years that's $880K in foregone guaranteed income. To break even on the equity (ignoring time value of money), I need the company to hit roughly $200M valuation at liquidity. That's a 4x from today's $50M post-money.

Honestly, 4x feels achievable for a company that has this level of product-market clarity, this team, and this investor. Series A companies that hit $200M+ aren't exceptional — they're just the ones that execute. And I'd be the person most responsible for the technical execution.

**What worries me**

The market timing feels right but the regulatory piece is something I don't fully understand. Legal services are regulated at the state level and the "lawyer working at a software company" model is unusual. I've asked about this and they've said it's structured correctly, but I haven't done independent diligence on it.

The other thing is I'd be leaving a team I love and a product used by a billion people for something that might not work. That's just startup risk and I've accepted it.

**My gut**

I think I'd regret not taking this more than I'd regret taking it and it failing. I've been at a big company for six years and I know what the next six years look like if I stay — more of the same, incrementally more comp, incrementally more scope. The Atrium opportunity is the kind of thing that comes around once every few years. Justin Kan sold Twitch for $970 million. He knows what he's doing.

I'm going to accept the offer.
